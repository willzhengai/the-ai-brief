Issue #18 November 16, 2026

Huang Out at Nvidia, the Outlook Outage, Anthropic's IPO Math, What Claude Fable 5.2 Can Do, and Two Org Briefings: the Flattened Middle and the AI-Enabled Fractional CFO

Editor's Take

Two headlines dominated last week, and neither was a product launch: Jensen Huang's abrupt exit from Nvidia, and a Microsoft outage that took Outlook down for most of a working day. I comment on both briefly below. Together with Anthropic's IPO filing, which puts $895.4B of compute commitments on the record, they make one point: the AI economy rests on very few people, platforms, and balance sheets.

Also this week: Claude Fable 5.2 is out, and I walk through what it can actually do. OpenAI gave its agents a wallet. And there are two Org briefings: the thinned middle-management layer that is carrying most AI rollouts, and a new look at org design for smaller companies, starting with how AI changes what a fractional CFO can deliver.

This Week's Stories

The Week's Two Loudest Headlines: Huang Out at Nvidia, and the Outlook Outage

SEC / Nvidia / SecurityWeek / Office 365 for IT Pros / CNN Industry

Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia. On November 12 the company filed an 8-K announcing that Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and director, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress, in the finance seat since 2013, is interim CEO and keeps the CFO role while the board runs a search that includes internal and external candidates. The filing gives no reason; the resignation followed press reports about Huang's personal conduct that have also drawn congressional attention. Press tallies count at least ten analyst downgrades on succession uncertainty and export-control risk. Kress's first earnings call as interim CEO is Tuesday, November 17.

My brief comment: the personal story will get the airtime, and the business story is the one that matters for anyone building on AI. Nvidia was the most founder-identified company in the AI supply chain. Its roadmap, its customer relationships, and its allocation decisions ran through one person, and the whole industry planned around that. Kress is the continuity choice and the right one for the next two quarters. The open question is who sets product direction after that, and no interim appointment answers it.

Microsoft's Outlook outage was not an AI story, and it still belongs here. Starting around 16:17 UTC on November 12, Exchange Online mailbox access failed, first across the Americas and then, by Microsoft's own later statement, potentially in any region. Outlook took the hardest hit; Teams and Copilot went down with it. Recovery took more than 12 hours and the incident (EX1550543) was reported resolved on November 13. Microsoft says it was a technical failure and ruled out a cyberattack. SecurityWeek reports the company traced it to an earlier network capacity upgrade, with a later network change exposing the problem; the full post-incident report is not out yet. Microsoft has not confirmed how many users were affected. Press reports run as high as 430 million.

My brief comment: two things. First, when the assistant lives inside the suite, an email outage is also an AI outage. Copilot was unavailable for the same hours Outlook was. Second, attackers moved faster than the fix: Microsoft's threat team flagged a phishing campaign using the incident ID as bait while the outage was still running. If your team received a "restore your mailbox" email last week, treat it as hostile.

Strategic read: both headlines are the same lesson. One company's direction depended on one person; a large share of the world's office work depended on one platform. Before the next one, write down three things: how the leadership team reaches each other when email and chat are both down, which AI workflows stop when your primary suite stops, and which of your own vendors has a single person you could not replace.
Sources: Nvidia 8-K (SEC) · Nvidia: CEO transition · Yahoo Finance: Wall Street on Kress · SecurityWeek: cause and phishing lure · Office 365 for IT Pros: outage timeline · CNN: scale of the outage

Anthropic's IPO Math: A $17B Quarter Under $895.4B of Compute Commitments

Anthropic / Yahoo Finance / gHacks Industry

Anthropic's S-1 is the first on-the-record look inside a frontier lab. The company filed on October 26 and amended the filing in early November. Third-quarter revenue came in at roughly $17B, and nine-month revenue at $33.2B. Growth is still fast and clearly decelerating: quarter-over-quarter growth was 46.7%, down from 144.2% the quarter before. The company swung to a $567M operating loss in Q3 after $974M of operating income in Q2, and attributed the swing mainly to $5.0B of compute bought under short-term commitments. The number that matters most is on the other side of the ledger: compute and infrastructure commitments of about $895.4B, up from $417B in the earlier draft, with $69.8B falling due within 12 months.

Run the ratio: $895.4B of commitments against Q3 revenue annualized at about $68B is roughly 13 years of current revenue (my arithmetic, not a filing figure). The bull case is that the commitments are staged over many years and revenue is compounding. The bear case is in the same document: growth is slowing while the short-term compute bill is already large enough to erase a quarter's operating profit.

The offering itself: Anthropic launched its roadshow on November 10 for 825 million Class A shares at an expected $70 to $78, listing on Nasdaq under "ANT". Press reports put pricing on November 18 and first trading on November 19; the company has not confirmed those dates. Valuation headlines range from about $916B to $1.02T on a market-cap basis to roughly $1.4T fully diluted, so check which basis any number you see is using. The timing is tight: Nvidia, whose chips sit under most of that compute, reports earnings under an interim CEO the day before the expected pricing (see the first story).

Strategic read: your AI vendors' cost structure is now public, and it tells you where pricing goes. A lab carrying commitments at 13 times annualized revenue has to keep usage-based billing and has limited room to discount the capacity you actually want. Three practical moves: put price-protection and portability terms into any multi-year AI contract you sign this quarter; keep a second model provider tested on your real workloads; and treat single-supplier concentration in your AI stack the way you would in any other critical supply chain. The counterargument: staged commitments with cancellation rights are less binding than the headline total implies, and one outlet reports that at least one contract in the filing is cancellable.
Sources: Anthropic: S-1 filing announcement · Anthropic: roadshow launch · gHacks: revised IPO filing · Yahoo Finance: the filing in 5 numbers · Yahoo Finance: roadshow and valuation

Claude Fable 5.2 Is Out: What It Can Do

Anthropic Industry

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 on November 9. The short version: it is built for long, loosely defined technical work, and the launch material describes people handing it a project and coming back days later. It runs ambiguous work for days, coordinates hundreds or even a thousand-plus subagents, and checks its own results as it goes. It is also cheaper and faster than the version it replaces: $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output (down from $10 and $50), with output up to 30% faster.

What that looks like in practice, from Anthropic's launch material:

  • Software at project scale. Starting from an empty folder, it coordinated more than 1,000 subagents for eight days and produced a prototype operating system that boots on a laptop. Inside Anthropic, a team of agents migrated a production service from Python to Rust; it now starts about 26 times faster.
  • Long unattended reviews. An Uber engineer describes a 10 to 12 hour security audit in which the model sent out about 170 reviewer agents and came back with a write-up, fix pull requests, and a policy proposal.
  • Finance and document work. Box reports that in a loan-application review it flagged that the salary deposits and the bank statement balance did not match, so the underwriter knew where to look. Anthropic describes finance reports "that could take a week of effort."
  • Physical-world design. From a one-paragraph prompt it designed three working electronic devices (circuit boards, enclosures, and software), each ready for manufacturing in under a day. It also shows a large jump in controlling robots.
  • Research. A team of 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents formalized the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 30 hours; an earlier research model needed 11 days.

What to keep in proportion. Every number above is Anthropic's own, including the benchmarks. Anthropic itself recommends starting most everyday coding and knowledge work on its cheaper Opus 5.5 and moving up to Fable only when the task demands it. The lead is not clean across the board: on Anthropic's own table, Google's Gemini 4 Carbon scores higher on the business-workflow benchmark (53.9% vs. 51.4%). Cybersecurity and biology questions are routed to less capable models by design; the unrestricted version (Mythos 5.2) is limited to vetted organizations. And Fable models carry 30-day data retention by default, which matters if you handle regulated data.

Strategic read: the capability that changed is duration. A model that holds a multi-day task, splits it across agents, and flags what it could not verify is a different tool from a chat assistant, and it fits work most firms have parked for lack of people: a system migration, a contract-set review, a data cleanup. Pick one parked project, scope it, and run it as a test. See this week's fractional-CFO briefing for what that means in a finance function.
Sources: Anthropic: Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 launch post · Anthropic: Claude Fable product page (pricing, benchmarks, customer quotes)

Agents Get a Wallet

OpenAI / CNBC / PYMNTS Industry

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on November 13. It lets the company's personal agents ("dots") make purchases for a user. Launch scope is narrow: ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 and over in the U.S. with eligible U.S.-issued cards. Two design choices are worth understanding. First, the agent never sees the card: it pays with network tokens from American Express, Mastercard, and Visa. Second, the user approves each transaction at launch; fully delegated purchasing with pre-approvals is planned for later. On the merchant side, OpenAI published a Commerce Partner Playbook that asks businesses to configure their CDN or firewall to recognize signed requests from ChatGPT agents through Web Bot Auth. Still open: merchant fees are undisclosed, and the launch materials do not fully address refunds, returns, or what happens when the agent buys the wrong thing.

Also in the model tier: Google's Gemini 4 Carbon became available November 10. Third-party reports put its introductory pricing at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output, rising to $4 and $20 after the introductory period. I could not confirm that pricing on a Google page, so verify it before you budget against it.

Strategic read: if you sell online, an agent is now a buyer, and your bot-blocking rules may be turning it away. Ask your web team one question this week: do we block, allow, or identify signed agent traffic? Then decide your returns policy for agent-initiated orders before the first dispute.
Sources: OpenAI: Introducing ChatGPT Wallet · CNBC: ChatGPT Wallet · PYMNTS: a wallet without the card · Lindy: Gemini 4 Carbon access and pricing (third-party)

The Flattened Middle Is Carrying Your AI Rollout

Two new surveys agree: companies are thinning the management layer that AI adoption depends on, and most cannot say whether the managers who remain are ready.

TL;DR

AI change is being delivered by middle managers, and almost nobody above them is checking whether they can deliver it. Korn Ferry's Talent Trends 2027 report found that only 6% of respondents believe their top executives are fully prepared to lead AI-driven transformation, and 66% expect managers to spend more of their time helping teams through change than overseeing daily work.

The managers are not ready either, and many firms do not know it. In a Paradigm survey of HR leaders at more than 650 companies, just 6% said their middle managers are ready to lead change companywide. Nearly one in five said managers are unprepared across the whole organization, and another 19% said they do not know.

Meanwhile the work itself has not been redesigned. Deloitte's AI Pulse Check (about 3,700 professionals) found 48% of organizations have introduced AI without redesigning the workflows or roles around it. Only 12% report redesign at scale with a new operating model behind it.

Two different 6% numbers, one problem

The coincidence is worth separating, because the two figures measure different things. Korn Ferry's 6% is about the top: how few people think senior executives are fully prepared to lead the transformation. Paradigm's 6% is about the middle: how few HR leaders think their managers are ready everywhere in the company. Put together, they describe a rollout where neither the layer setting the mandate nor the layer delivering it is judged ready, and the weight lands on the layer with less authority.

Korn Ferry's Cathi Rittelmann has the phrase for it: "Managers will become the shock absorbers of the organization." Her colleague Susan De La Vega describes the mechanism to Fortune: managers end up defending and communicating AI mandates they had no say in and little context for. That is the job most companies are now asking of a group they are simultaneously shrinking. HR Executive's reporting on the "Great Flattening" makes the same point from the other direction: the layer is being cut in the name of AI efficiency without preparing the managers who remain for wider spans and a change-leadership job.

Why this is the binding constraint

Deloitte's data explains why the middle matters more than the tooling. Nearly half of organizations have dropped AI into existing workflows and roles unchanged. Value shows up when the workflow is rebuilt, which Deloitte describes as redesigning the "work chart" (how work, decisions, and accountability are organized) ahead of the org chart. Workflow redesign is local, detailed work. It is done by the people who know how the work actually runs, which in most companies means the managers one or two levels above the front line. Remove them first and you have removed the people who would do the redesign.

The firms making progress follow a recognizable pattern. Among organizations that are changing how work is done, 37% start by fully owning one workflow, testing it, and then scaling.

What to do with this

  1. Measure manager readiness before you cut the layer. A fifth of HR leaders cannot say whether their managers are ready. Get a function-by-function read first; it is cheaper than discovering the gap after the reorg.
  2. Brief managers before their teams, with the reasoning. A manager who hears the AI mandate at the same time as their team cannot explain it, defend it, or adapt it. Give them the why, the metric, and the room to object a week early.
  3. Rewrite the manager job on paper. If two-thirds of the role is now leading change, something has to come off. Status reporting, routine approvals, and schedule coordination are the obvious candidates to hand to agents. Say so explicitly, in the role description and in how managers are measured.
  4. Redesign one workflow end to end, with a manager as its owner. Follow the 37%: pick one process, give one manager authority over it, rebuild it with AI inside, measure it, then scale the method.
  5. Sequence delayering after redesign. Wider spans are sustainable once the workflow carries less coordination overhead. Widening them before the redesign adds load to the people doing the redesign.
The strongest counterargument: flattening is the forcing function, and organizations never redesign work until the old structure is gone. That holds where senior leaders can run the redesign themselves. The Korn Ferry number says most cannot yet. Until they can, the middle layer is the only place the redesign capacity exists, and spending it on absorbing shocks is the expensive choice.
Sources: Korn Ferry: Talent Trends 2027 · Fortune: "shock absorbers" · Fast Company: the Paradigm survey · HR Executive: the "Great Flattening" · Deloitte: AI Pulse Check

Second Briefing: Org Design for SMBs

The Fractional CFO, Rebuilt: What AI Changes for SMBs and the Lower Mid-Market

The fractional CFO used to be strategy without the weeds. A capable CFO with capable AI can now work from the raw data up, and that changes the org design of a small company's finance function.

TL;DR

The old problem: most fractional CFOs are strategic and stay at the top of the stack. They polish what the books already say. Few get into how data actually flows into the financial system, because on a part-time engagement that work needed either a team of analysts or the full cooperation of the existing controller.

What AI changes: a CFO who can use AI well can go into the messy source data personally. AI can map how the systems should connect, reconcile them, and clean the data, in days instead of a staffing plan.

The new regime: a small company can now get someone who has been there and seen that, whose advice rests on its real operating data. That combination used to require a full-time CFO plus a finance team.

Why the fractional CFO was hard to make work

The pitch has always been attractive: senior judgment at a fraction of the cost. The delivery has often disappointed, for a structural reason. Good decisions depend on trustworthy numbers, and in most small and mid-sized companies the numbers are the problem. Revenue lives in one system, billing in another, payroll in a third, and the general ledger is stitched together with spreadsheets and habit.

A part-time CFO facing that had three options, all poor:

  1. Stay on top. Take the books as given and add a layer: a board deck, a forecast, a KPI page. This is top polishing. It looks strategic and inherits every error underneath it.
  2. Bring an army. Staff the cleanup with analysts or an outsourced accounting team. It works, and it turns a fractional engagement into a consulting project with a consulting budget.
  3. Depend on the controller. Ask the existing controller or bookkeeper to pull, explain, and restructure the data. Some help generously. Many have little incentive to make it convenient for a part-time executive to inspect their work.

The standard advice in the fractional market reflects this. Practitioner guides tell owners to keep the CFO out of the books and fix the close first, on the reasoning that otherwise the retainer gets spent on bookkeeping at CFO rates. That advice was sensible under the old economics. It also guaranteed that the most experienced person in the finance function was the furthest from the data.

What AI changes

The constraint was labor: tracing a number from the P&L back to its source transactions across three or four systems took days of someone's time. That is now work an AI model does well and quickly, under supervision. A capable CFO can connect read-only to the ledger, the bank feeds, billing, and payroll, and have AI:

  1. Map the plumbing. Document how data moves between systems today, where it is rekeyed by hand, and where the definitions disagree (what counts as a customer, a booking, a margin).
  2. Reconcile and clean. Match records across systems, surface the breaks, and propose corrections for a human to approve.
  3. Rebuild the reporting on top. Once the data ties out, the forecast and the KPI page rest on something real.

This week's Claude Fable 5.2 release shows where the capability stands. Box reports the model flagging a mismatch between salary deposits and a bank statement balance in a loan file. Oracle reports that in one test, two other models generated SQL that ran cleanly and overstated totals five to eight times by summing summary rows. The first example is why this works. The second is why the CFO still matters: the output has to be checked by someone who knows what the number should roughly be.

The org design that follows

For a company between roughly $5M and $100M in revenue, the finance function changes shape:

  1. The CFO seat can be fractional and still be deep. The test for the hire changes. Ask a candidate to show, in your systems, how they would trace one number from the P&L to source using AI in their first 30 days. A candidate who answers with a deck is the old model.
  2. The controller's job moves from gatekeeper to owner of controls. The controller no longer decides what the CFO gets to see. They own the close, the approvals, and the accuracy of what AI proposes. That is a better job, and it should be offered that way.
  3. The analyst layer shrinks or never gets hired. The work a first finance analyst did (pulling, matching, formatting) is the work AI absorbs first.
  4. Guardrails are part of the design. Read-only access first. AI proposes entries and a person posts them. Reconcile to the bank every period as the independent check. Use enterprise AI tools with clear data terms for payroll and customer data. And document the pipelines, so the function does not depend on one fractional executive's laptop. This week's headlines are a reminder of what single points of failure cost.
The strongest counterargument: AI cleans data; it does not fix the process that produces bad data. If orders are entered wrong at the source, the cleanup repeats every month until someone changes the process, and that is operating work a part-time executive may not have the hours for. The combination is also scarce. CFO Dive, citing BTG's 2026 talent report, has interim-CFO demand up 14% year over year, with "financial controls, accounting and audit" the most requested skills and digital and AI work touching a quarter of requests. Experienced CFOs who are also fluent with AI are a small pool, and for now they can choose their clients.
