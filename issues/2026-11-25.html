Issue #19 November 25, 2026

Nvidia's Eleven Days, Anthropic Below Its IPO Price, Banks Shut the Door on Agents, and Two Org Briefings: AI Is Cutting Hiring Before Jobs, and the Fractional CMO Rebuilt

Editor's Take

Last issue closed with two open questions: how Nvidia would report without Jensen Huang, and how Anthropic's IPO would land. Both got answered within a week, and in both cases the operating numbers were the least important part of the answer. Nvidia beat consensus by roughly $6.7B and raised its outlook, then reversed its own CEO decision six days after saying it would not. Anthropic priced the largest IPO on record above its range, opened 34% up, and closed below its offer price on its fourth trading day. Nothing in either company's business changed between those prints.

The lesson for operators is the one from last week, sharpened: the AI economy is priced on a few people and a few narratives, and both can swing faster than any procurement cycle. Plan on the operating facts (Nvidia's supply, Anthropic's growth and cost structure) and treat the price action as noise.

Also this week: the big U.S. banks have started refusing customers' AI agents, a week after OpenAI gave its agents a wallet. And two Org briefings. First, Anthropic published two studies on what AI is doing to jobs, one inside its own walls and one across 58 customers. The headline is that hiring is slowing while layoffs stay rare, and the finding that matters more is that light-touch AI stays low value. Second, the SMB org-design series that started with last issue's fractional CFO moves to marketing: the fractional CMO used to write the plan and hand the work to an agency, and AI lets one person own both again. Issue #12 covered the enterprise CMO's AI decision; this is the small-company version.

This Week's Stories

Nvidia's Eleven Days: A Record Quarter, Then a CEO Reversal

Nvidia / CBS / Yahoo Finance / Axios Industry

The quarter was not the story. On November 17, in its first report under interim CEO Colette Kress, Nvidia posted Q3 fiscal 2027 revenue of $115.8B, up 103% year over year and 20% sequentially. Data center revenue was $107.6B. Non-GAAP gross margin was 74.9%. Pre-report Bloomberg consensus was $109.15B, so the beat was about $6.7B (my arithmetic). Q4 guidance is about $130B, plus or minus 3%, with two caveats in the release: the quarter has an extra week, and the outlook assumes no China data center compute revenue. Vera Rubin NVL72 has shipped, which Nvidia calls the fastest ramp in its history; per an unofficial call transcript, Kress put first-quarter production shipments at nearly $20B. On the same call, board chair Harvey Jones said the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO."

Six days later, it did. On Monday, November 23, Nvidia announced that its board had voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, CEO, and director, effective immediately. The release says the board "received input from senior levels of the United States government" and is confident the reinstatement "poses no national security risk." President Trump told CNBC he had spoken with the board directly. The independent investigation, opened after reports that Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, continues "to conclusion." The board added a National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors and opened a search for a Chief Compliance Officer. Kress returns to the CFO seat. The stock closed up 31.5% at $165.05, adding nearly $1T in market value, and gave back part of it the next day.

My brief comment: last week I wrote that Kress was the right continuity choice and that no interim appointment answers who sets product direction. The board answered it by going back. That removes the succession question and replaces it with a governance one: a board said one thing on an earnings call and the opposite six days later, after government input, with its own investigation still open. The market paid nearly $1T for the founder's return in a day. That is the clearest price anyone has put on key-person risk in this cycle.

Strategic read: for buyers of AI capacity, very little changed in practice. Supply is still the constraint, Vera Rubin is shipping, and the roadmap Kress described is the one Huang inherits. What changed is the risk register. The open investigation, congressional scrutiny of export compliance, and the new compliance committee are now standing items for anyone with multi-year GPU or cloud commitments that depend on Nvidia. Keep supply terms and second-source options on the agenda for your next infrastructure review, whatever the stock does. The counterargument: a founder-led Nvidia is the known quantity, and the market's verdict suggests the bigger risk was the version without him.

Anthropic Prices the Largest IPO on Record, Then Trades Below It

CBS / TheStreet / Admirals / Fast Company Industry

The deal went better than marketed. Anthropic priced on November 18 at $83 a share, above the $70 to $78 range, and upsized the offering from 825 million to 980 million Class A shares. That raised $81.3B, ahead of SpaceX's $75B, plus a $26B concurrent placement of non-voting shares sold at the IPO price to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Microsoft, and Nvidia. At $83 the company was valued at about $1.5T fully diluted, or about $1.1T on shares outstanding; check the basis on any number you see. ANT opened on Nasdaq on November 19 at $111.50, up 34%, and closed at $97.17, up 17%, on a day the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.3%.

Then the market tested the thesis. The stock slid for three sessions and, per Admirals, closed at $81.91 on November 24, its first close below the IPO price. TheStreet reported an intraday drop of about 13% to roughly $76 that morning. The reasons cited are the ones last issue's S-1 numbers raised: stretched AI valuations and the cost of the infrastructure behind them. Two supply facts add to it. Form 4 filings show Dario and Daniela Amodei each sold 3,378,378 shares in the offering at $83, about $280M each, and TheStreet reports all seven co-founders were scheduled to sell the same amount, about $1.96B combined. Roughly 303 million more shares become eligible for sale on December 15 and again on January 5, per Barron's as cited by TheStreet. On the other side, Canaccord initiated at Buy with a $135 target and Oppenheimer at Outperform with $150.

My brief comment: four trading days took the stock from a roughly $2T fully diluted valuation at the open back through its offer price, while nothing in the business changed. The investors who set the $83 price had read the same S-1: $895.4B of compute commitments, growth decelerating from 144% to 47% quarter over quarter. Day one priced the growth. Day four priced the commitments. Both are in the same document.

Strategic read: for Anthropic's customers, the listing matters more than the price. Your vendor now reports quarterly, under public-market pressure on margins, with a compute bill that already erased one quarter's operating profit. Expect pricing discipline and few discounts, and expect the most capable tiers to stay usage-priced. Two moves: read the first quarterly report for gross margin and commitment pacing before you sign anything multi-year, and keep a second provider tested on real workloads. The counterargument: a busted first week says little about a company growing at this rate, and the record of large tech IPOs that broke issue early and compounded afterward is long.
Sources: CBS: Nasdaq debut · Admirals: first day of trading · Admirals: below the IPO price · TheStreet via Yahoo Finance: founder sales and lockups · Fast Company: why ANT dipped

The Banks Shut the Door on AI Agents

Hunton Andrews Kurth Industry

Last week agents got a wallet. This week the banks drew a line. Wells Fargo's rewritten Online Access Agreement took effect on November 24. A new Section 15 on third-party AI agents says customers use them "at your own risk," treats every agent-initiated transaction as authorized by the customer, disclaims any duty to monitor agent activity, and reserves the right to cut agent access at any time. Wells is the permissive one. JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, and Charles Schwab moved in mid-October to block agents that log in with customer credentials; Schwab says it blocks unauthorized agents trying to move client money 10 to 20 times a week. JPMorgan's updated agreement, effective January 1, 2027, also treats an agent's instructions as the customer's own. All four still allow read-only access through open-banking connectors. The trigger, per Hunton, was the launch of Meta's Muse personal assistant.

My brief comment: read the Wells Fargo clause next to OpenAI's design for ChatGPT Wallet. OpenAI built around network tokens and per-purchase approval precisely so the agent never holds the keys. The banks are now saying that if an agent does hold the keys, every mistake is yours. The industry is converging on one rule: agents may pay through the card networks and read through open banking, and may not log in as you.

Strategic read: if your finance team is piloting agents for treasury, payables, or reconciliation, check how they authenticate. An agent using a person's banking credentials is now either blocked or carrying unlimited liability for the company. Route agent work through read-only connectors and tokenized payment rails, keep a human on money movement, and ask your bank what sanctioned agent access it plans to offer and when.

AI Is Cutting Hiring Before Jobs: What Anthropic's Two Workforce Studies Show

One study looks inside the most AI-intensive company there is; the other interviews 58 of its customers. Both say the same thing: AI is cutting hiring before it cuts jobs, and the value goes to firms that reorganize.

TL;DR

Few layoffs, much less hiring. In "Working with AI" (November 23), Anthropic's economists interviewed 66 people at 58 U.S. companies using Claude. They found "very few examples of domestic layoffs" driven by AI. People at two-thirds of the companies reported reduced hiring because of it.

The junior rung goes first. Inside Anthropic itself ("How employment is changing at Anthropic," October 16), the junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%, while agents logged six times as many hours as the employees directing them.

Value follows reorganization. "The highest-value applications involve wide-reaching organizational change." Light-touch uses that fit the existing structure were often rated low value.

What the customer study found

The headline quote is from an interviewee: "We may not be firing, but we're not hiring." Only two companies saw AI fully eliminate a position and cost someone a job; five pushed out employees who would not adopt AI; ten were hiring more because of AI, mostly for implementation and IT support. About a third of the hiring reductions came from letting functions shrink through attrition. Most came from growth that no longer needed new people. As one engineering leader put it: "We've added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people." Six startups estimated they would need about four times as many employees without AI.

The early job losses are outside the walls. Nine companies cut contracted work (software development, customer service, legal, back office), much of it already offshore. The authors flag this as a possible early warning for the international business-services industry.

Most applications created value by raising output; few did it by cutting cost. The bottlenecks were internal: reviewing AI output, other functions failing to keep pace with faster coding, uneven adoption, and missing context or data infrastructure. Customer skepticism and regulation were rarely cited.

What Anthropic's own workforce shows

The internal study is the leading indicator, since Anthropic runs further ahead on agents than any customer. Beyond the junior-hiring drop and the six-to-one agent hours, three numbers stand out. Claude sessions spend about 80% of their time waiting for human input. The share of employees' sessions spent managing agents rose from 10% to 22% in six months. And for non-technical teams, reading the AI's output would take over half the work week. The constraint has moved from producing work to directing and reviewing it.

The org chart moved with it. The technical share of employment fell from 61% to 56%. Employees with a year of tenure were 50% more likely to change departments each month than a year earlier, and whole teams were reassigned as agents took over their work. Median meeting time rose 20%. A majority of managers said they now hire for a different skill set, weighting autonomy and judgment over domain expertise.

Top-down or bottom-up

The customer study's most useful finding for anyone running a rollout is that neither direction works alone. Top-down automation met expert resistance, surveillance fears, and brittle "zombie" apps. Bottom-up use created pockets of value that stalled. A hotel operations director explained why: "The individual doesn't have the authority to change the work. They can only apply it in their own area." This is the same constraint last issue's briefing found in the middle-management layer: the redesign needs someone with authority over the whole workflow.

What to do with this

  1. Decide the junior pipeline on purpose. If AI is quietly absorbing entry-level hiring, you are also cutting the bench your future managers come from. Set an explicit number of junior hires per function and a plan for what they learn, instead of letting it fall to zero by default.
  2. Budget review capacity, not just licenses. If 80% of an agent's time is waiting on a person and reading its output takes half a week, review is the new bottleneck. Name who reviews what, and measure turnaround.
  3. Pair top-down mandate with bottom-up ownership. Give one person authority over an entire workflow (the authority bottom-up users lack) and let the people doing the work redesign it (the knowledge top-down programs lack).
  4. Plan for mobility. At Anthropic, people and whole teams moved as agents took over their work. Build the redeployment path before you need it; Anthropic says it has set up an early-warning system for roles at risk.
  5. Watch your contractors first. If AI-driven cuts start anywhere in your company, the study suggests outsourced and offshore work is where. Review those contracts and the knowledge they hold before they lapse.
The strongest counterargument: both studies come from a company that sells the product and just listed on Nasdaq, and neither is representative. The customer interviews are qualitative, drawn from Anthropic's own enterprise customers, and the authors say so. The internal study says its hiring and agent-hours findings are descriptive and do not establish that AI caused them. Treat them as a direction of travel. That direction is still the useful part: hiring slows before layoffs arrive, and that is the window to redesign roles while it is still a choice.
Sources: Anthropic: Working with AI (58 companies) · Anthropic: How employment is changing at Anthropic · eWeek: agents log six times staff hours

Second Briefing: Org Design for SMBs

The Fractional CMO, Rebuilt: From Strategy Deck to Running the Machine

The fractional CMO used to write the plan and hand it to an agency. With AI doing the execution volume, the plan and the work can sit with one person again, and the marketing function of a small company changes shape.

TL;DR

The old problem: a fractional CMO sets positioning, picks channels, and writes the plan. Someone else executes it: an agency, freelancers, or one overloaded marketing coordinator. The strategy and the results live in different firms, and nobody owns the gap.

What AI changes: the execution layer (drafts, variants, campaign builds, reporting) is the part of marketing AI absorbs first. A CMO who can use AI well can run the work, not just specify it, and can start from the company's own customer data instead of a brand workshop.

The new regime: one experienced marketer, part time, with AI and a small in-house operator, can now cover what used to take a fractional CMO plus an agency retainer. The money moves from production to judgment and measurement.

Why the fractional CMO was hard to make work

Demand is real. In a survey of 113 fractional marketing leaders by The Slice Network and Robert Half, 45% said demand rose over the past year, and the top reason clients give is senior expertise without a long-term commitment (34%), well ahead of cost (19%). Scale-ups and early-growth firms account for nearly 80% of it. The same survey shows the strain: 55% of fractional CMOs fill less than half their available time, and 38% take home under £5,000 a month. The role is in demand and hard to make pay.

The structural reason mirrors the fractional CFO. A part-time marketing leader at a $5M to $100M company had three options, all poor:

  1. Stay on top. Deliver positioning, a channel plan, and a quarterly deck. It reads as strategic and depends entirely on whoever executes it.
  2. Bring an agency. It works, and the retainer often costs more than the CMO. The CMO becomes an agency manager, and the agency's incentives (more production, more hours) are not the company's.
  3. Depend on the team. Hand the plan to an internal coordinator who is already running the website, the newsletter, and trade-show logistics. The plan waits.

Underneath all three sits the same data problem the CFO faces. Lead source is defined three ways across the CRM, the ad platforms, and the web analytics. Nobody can say which channel produced last quarter's best customers. Mid-market marketers feel it: in WARC and Mailchimp data on firms with 10 to 499 employees, 26% are unsure how to measure the return on their AI use, and 39% name lack of skills as the top barrier.

What AI changes

The constraint was labor, again. Production and analysis took hands, and hands came from an agency or a hire. That is the work AI does well under supervision. A capable CMO with read access to the CRM, the ad accounts, and web analytics can have AI:

  1. Rebuild the customer picture from data. Pull closed-won deals, trace them back through their touchpoints, and find which segments and channels actually produce good customers. This replaces the persona workshop with evidence.
  2. Run production in-house. Drafts, ad variants, landing pages, email sequences, and sales enablement at a volume that used to need an agency, edited and approved by the CMO.
  3. Close the loop on measurement. Reconcile what the platforms claim against what the CRM shows, weekly, so spend follows results rather than reports.

Buyers are already moving. Forrester's 2026 B2B brand and communications survey found the share of marketers expecting to increase digital agency spend fell from 51% to 31% in a year, and for content creation from 41% to 26%, as AI lets teams bring work in-house. In Marketing Week's 2025 State of B2B Marketing survey (450 marketers), 15% had already cut agency spend because of AI and 2% had raised it, and small firms were more likely than large ones to say AI freed them for more complex work (51% versus 41%).

The org design that follows

For a company between roughly $5M and $100M in revenue, the marketing function changes shape:

  1. The CMO seat can be fractional and still run the work. Change the hiring test. Ask a candidate to show, in your CRM, how they would find your ten best customers' common path in their first 30 days, using AI. A candidate who answers with a brand framework is the old model.
  2. Hire one operator before you sign one agency. The first in-house marketing hire should be a marketing operations generalist who owns the CRM, the automation, and the AI tooling, not a content writer.
  3. Keep agencies for what is scarce, not what is volume. Media buying at scale, brand identity, and specialist creative still justify outside help. Routine production and reporting no longer do.
  4. Guardrails are part of the design. Read-only data access first. A human approves anything customer-facing. Reconcile platform numbers to the CRM every month as the independent check. Write a short voice and claims guide so AI output stays on-brand and legally safe. And keep the prompts, workflows, and data connections in company accounts, so the function survives the fractional leader's departure.
The strongest counterargument: production was never the bottleneck; distribution and differentiation were. If everyone can make ten times the content, the marginal piece is worth less, and a fractional CMO running an AI content machine may just add volume to a crowded feed. The CMSWire reporting on this year's CMO job warns against exactly that, and The CMO Survey (308 senior US marketers) finds no marketing-technology capability scoring above 5 of 7, with no improvement in two years. The answer is in how the role is used: the gain is in the first and third steps above (knowing which customers pay, and proving what works), with production a means. A fractional CMO who sells volume is the old model with cheaper inputs.
